What the El Niño forecast actually says

The Los Angeles Times laid out the forecast on November 13. NOAA's Climate Prediction Center puts the odds that this El Niño ends up "very strong" at about 100%, and gives it a 60% chance of being the strongest on record since 1950. NOAA's outlook gives Southern California a 60% to 70% chance of above-normal rain from January through March.

A National Weather Service meteorologist called it "not a slam dunk," and in the big 2015-16 El Niño the south coast actually got only 77% of normal rain. The forecasters were more confident about the coast than the rain: coastal flooding and beach erosion were the high-confidence risks, with king tides expected around Thanksgiving, Christmas and MLK Day. Sea levels in Southern California are already running about 16 inches above what the tides alone would predict, and records were broken in October at San Pedro, San Diego Bay and La Jolla.

We got a preview last week. The National Weather Service put a Coastal Flood Advisory on all of coastal Orange County for November 9 and 10, with high tides of 7.2 to 7.4 feet, about 1.2 to 1.4 feet above predictions (Patch). Waves were hitting homes in Capistrano Beach at high tide earlier this month.

San Clemente: still a 14-day market, and mostly outside flood zones

Here's the part that should calm some nerves. MLS data compiled by RealaTrends shows San Clemente homes went under contract in a median of 14 days over the 12 months to October 30, faster than 21 days the year before. The 12-month median sale price was $1,797,000, up 4.8%.

Of the San Clemente homes for sale on October 30, none were in a FEMA Special Flood Hazard Area. The issues here tend to be slopes, drainage and the beach, not river-style flooding. 73 homes for sale were in an earthquake-induced landslide zone, and 60 were in a Very High Fire Hazard Severity Zone, which is a good reminder that a wet winter after a fire season can mean runoff and erosion near the canyons.

Dana Point and Capistrano Beach: strong prices, more coastal exposure

Dana Point's median sale price was $2,077,500 over the 90 days through November 6, up 9.2% from a year earlier, with homes selling in a median of 21 days at 98.7% of original list price. Nearly half of buyers, 48%, paid cash (MLS data via RealaTrends).

Dana Point does have more homes near the water. Of 116 homes for sale on November 13, 9 were in a FEMA flood hazard area and 5 were in a tsunami hazard area. In Capistrano Beach, Redfin's September data showed a median sale price of about $2.07 million, but homes took a median of 46 days to sell, at 97.4% of list, and 28.1% had a price drop. Buyers are paying strong prices, but asking more questions.

Orange County: inventory holding, buyers negotiating at the top

The Orange County Housing Report for November 9 showed 4,912 active listings, 509 new listings in a week and 1,445 homes in escrow. Above $2.5 million, sellers closed an average of 7% below asking that week, about $361,000. The report noted that's a small number of luxury sales, so I wouldn't read too much into one week. But on a $3 million home, 7% is about $210,000 (my own math). At the top end, anything that gives a buyer a reason to worry, like a damp crawlspace or an undocumented repair, tends to show up in the offer.

If you're selling this winter, here's what I'd do

  • Check your parcel, not the headlines. Your Natural Hazard Disclosure will tell buyers whether your home is in a flood, fire or seismic hazard zone (Civil Code section 1103). Pull it early so nothing surprises you or the buyer.
  • Let the rain work for you. Clean gutters and drains and walk the property after the first big storm. A home that stays dry through an El Niño winter is a selling point. Take photos.
  • Document, don't hide. If you've had a leak or water in the past, disclose it with the repair receipts. Buyers forgive a fixed problem far more easily than one their inspector finds. (I'm not a lawyer; for anything tricky, ask a real estate attorney.)
  • Winter can be a good time to list. Fewer homes compete for the buyers who are still out, and those buyers tend to be serious.
  • Investors and landlords: California leases already require a flood hazard disclosure if you know the home is in a flood area, plus a note recommending renter's and flood insurance (Government Code section 8589.45). Worth a look before you sign a new tenant this winter.

Rates fell for a fourth straight week

Freddie Mac's survey for November 12 put the 30-year fixed at 7.11%, down from 7.18%, and the 15-year at 6.45%. A year ago the 30-year was 6.24%. The Orange County Housing Report called a rate that starts with a six "the number to watch" heading into winter.

On San Clemente's $1,797,000 median with 20% down, principal and interest on a $1,437,600 loan is about $9,671 a month at 7.11%, about $68 less than a week ago. At 6.99% it would be about $9,555, roughly $116 less again. It's still about $829 a month more than at last year's 6.24%. (My own math, principal and interest only.)

San Clemente: rail plan approved, Measure M losing

The California Coastal Commission unanimously approved OCTA's $305 million plan to protect the rail corridor through San Clemente. It includes 690,000 cubic yards of sand for San Clemente beaches, a 1,200-foot seawall at San Clemente State Beach, and keeping about 48,000 tons of boulders already placed, plus 2,100 tons more (ABC7). Critics worry the rock will shrink the public beach. For homeowners, more sand on the beach is good news.

Meanwhile, Measure M, the proposed 1-cent sales tax for sand and wildfire prevention, was losing 62.5% to 37.5% in the count updated November 11 (Decision Desk HQ). If you want sandbags before the next storm, the city has offered free sand, bags and shovels at San Gorgonio Park and Richard T. Steed Memorial Park.

My take this week

  • Don't panic, prepare. The market is still moving, and most San Clemente homes for sale are outside FEMA flood zones.
  • Near the water or a slope? Know your hazard zones, fix the drainage and keep receipts. It protects your price.
  • Rates are easing. The Orange County Housing Report expects a rate starting with a six could bring hesitant buyers back.
